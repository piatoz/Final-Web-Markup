Digging deeper into the exposure triangle

  • Aperture

    The aperture is the physical opening inside the lens that controls how much light enters the camera body. It's measured in f-numbers like f/1.8 or f/8, and the number works backwards: a smaller number means a bigger opening and more light. Aperture also changes how much of your photo is in focus. A wide opening gives a blurry background, which is great for portraits, and a narrow opening keeps everything sharp, which suits landscapes.

    Lower f-number (f/1.8) = brighter photo + blurrier background. Higher f-number (f/8) = darker photo + everything in focus.

  • Shutter Speed

    Shutter speed is how long the camera's sensor is exposed to incoming light when you take a picture. It's measured in fractions of a second, like 1/1000 or 1/30. A fast shutter speed freezes movement, which is useful for sports, pets, or kids. A slow shutter speed lets in more light and blurs anything that moves, which can look creative for things like flowing water, but it also makes handheld photos blurry if your hands shake.

    Faster speed (1/1000) = freezes fast action + lets in less light. Slower speed (1/30) = creates motion blur + lets in more light.

  • ISO

    ISO is the digital brightness amplifier applied to the light gathered by your aperture and shutter speed. A low ISO gives clean, sharp photos but needs plenty of light. hen it gets dark, you can raise the ISO to brighten the image without changing the other two settings. The trade-off is digital grain (also called noise), which gets stronger as ISO goes up. That's why ISO is best used as a last resort, after you've adjusted aperture and shutter speed.

    Lower ISO (100) = cleaner, sharper image for bright daylight. Higher ISO (3200) = brightens dark night shots, but adds digital grain/noise.

Finding the right exposure balance

  • 1

    Pick your lead setting first

    Identify the most important element of your shot. Moving subjects? Shutter speed leads. Want blur or deep focus? Aperture leads. Low light? Set ISO first.

  • 2

    Keep ISO as low as possible

    Treat ISO as your safety net. Set your base ISO (100 or 160) first, and only raise it when the other two settings can't make the image bright enough.

  • 3

    The give-and-take rule

    The exposure triangle is a zero-sum game: if you let in less light with one setting, you must gain it back with another to keep brightness identical.
